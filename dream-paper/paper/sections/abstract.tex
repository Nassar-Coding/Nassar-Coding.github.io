City agencies use short-horizon forecasts of 311 service-request volume
to plan daily service capacity, but forecast accuracy is only a proxy:
what matters is the allocation the forecast produces. We build a
reproducible benchmark from 36.8 million acquired service-request records (30.2 million retained after documented exclusions) across
four U.S. cities (New York, Chicago, San Francisco, Austin; 2020--2025),
harmonized into eight documented service families and joined with NOAA
station weather, and we evaluate forecasters jointly with the
capacity-allocation decisions they drive in a stylized, backlog-aware
daily staffing simulation under scarce, moderate, and generous capacity.
Calendar augmentation reduces next-day error in every city
(8.6--14.0\%, all $p \le 0.0005$); weather helps three cities and adds
exactly nothing in the fourth, a retained negative result. Pooled
cross-city training mostly hurts, and zero-shot transfer degrades error
by up to 147\%. Downstream, the mapping from accuracy to decision value
is strongly regime-dependent: under scarcity, upgrading the allocation
policy (proportional $\rightarrow$ expected-value greedy) is worth more
than upgrading the forecaster, accuracy gains translate into decision
gains worth 2.6--13.7 percentage points of the achievable range, and quantile
forecasts beat point forecasts inside the same policy in all four
cities. Accuracy rankings and decision rankings correlate strongly
($\rho = 0.73$--$0.98$) yet selecting models by validation decision loss
instead of validation MAE changes the selected model in 7 of 12
city-regime settings. All data are public, all results regenerate from
one command, and the simulation makes no claim about any city's actual
operations.
