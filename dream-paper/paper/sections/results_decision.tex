All simulations run at budgets frozen from training data (New York
119/153/187 units across the scarce/moderate/generous regimes; Chicago
29/38/46; San Francisco 26/33/41; Austin 8/11/13; $\kappa=50$), under
the neutral equal-weights objective. Losses are waiting-time-weighted
unresolved stocks (Section~3); policy contrasts carry paired 28-day
block-bootstrap intervals on the daily loss differentials. Because the
trend diagnostic of Section~8 finds 45 of the 48 differential series
trend-dominated (the carryover stock compounds policy differences), we
read these intervals as \emph{descriptive characterizations of a
systematic, partly deterministic divergence within the simulation},
retaining a stationary-inference reading only where the diagnostic
supports it (Chicago, generous regime). The simulation itself is
deterministic given the frozen inputs, so every directional statement
below is exact for this environment; what the trending differentials
preclude is generalizing the interval widths as stationary sampling
uncertainty. Four findings emerge.

\paragraph{Point forecasts disable the expected-value optimizer; a
simple proportional rule is competitive with even its distribution-aware
form.}
Two separate statements, with separate strength. First, feeding
\emph{point} forecasts into the expected-value optimizer is a failure
mode: it loses to naive proportional apportionment in all 12
city-regime settings. The mechanism is identified and general: under a
degenerate forecast distribution the optimizer's marginal-value profile
is piecewise flat --- equal to $\kappa\omega$ for every unit below the
point estimate plus carryover, and zero for every unit above it --- so
under a neutral objective the greedy ordering carries no information in
either the deficit or the surplus segment, and allocation is decided by
tie-breaking (worked examples for both segments are in the supplement).
Second, against the \emph{fair} comparator --- the full-distribution arm
of the same quantile model, whose tails restore an allocation gradient
--- proportional apportionment remains competitive to slightly better:
lower loss in 11 of 12 settings by 0.1--6.5\%, with one reversal (San
Francisco, generous). This residual edge is an observed pattern for
which we do not claim a demonstrated mechanism; a plausible candidate
--- per-day-myopic optimality is not horizon optimality under
persistent carryover, and proportional apportionment approximates a
smoother hedge --- is left as a conjecture. Relative to the
forecast-free uniform floor, the proportional policy with the selected
forecaster reduces the cumulative unresolved stock by 18--23\%
(scarce), 38--70\% (moderate), and 60--93\% (generous), the regime
pattern itself being partly mechanical: under structural under-capacity
no policy can prevent stock growth, so policy differences compress.
All of this is a property of the simulated environment and its neutral
objective --- under the normative-priority sensitivity the gap
compresses --- and constitutes no guidance about any city's actual
operations.

\paragraph{Within the optimization family, the full distribution beats
its own point representations everywhere.}
Holding the single fitted quantile model and every other input fixed,
the full-distribution arm outperforms both the median arm and the
implied-mean arm in 12 of 12 settings, with all paired intervals
excluding zero. Distributional information breaks the flat-profile ties
that disable the point-forecast optimizer --- evidence that \emph{how} a
forecast enters a policy can matter as much as forecast accuracy
itself. One caveat is owed: the distribution whose value is measured is
itself imperfect --- its 90\% intervals cover only 77--81\% of outcomes
(Section~6) --- so this result demonstrates the value of the
\emph{available, under-dispersed} predictive distribution in this
simulation, not of calibrated uncertainty; it is plausibly a lower
bound on the latter.

\paragraph{Forecast quality transfers to simulated decision value within
a fixed policy.}
Replacing the trailing-mean baseline with the validation-selected
forecaster, policy held fixed, lowers the cumulative unresolved stock in
all 12 settings (average daily reduction in unresolved \emph{stock}
from $\approx$1.8 thousand request-equivalents in Austin to
$\approx$72 thousand in New York under scarcity). Consistently, model
\emph{rankings} by test MAE and by simulated decision loss agree
closely across the eleven-configuration grid (Spearman $\rho =
0.88$--$0.99$ in every city-regime cell): in this environment, accuracy
is largely a sufficient proxy for simulated decision value among
reasonable models.

\paragraph{Accuracy-based and decision-based selection mostly agree.}
Selecting over the cross-scope eleven-configuration grid by validation
decision loss instead of validation MAE --- both under the frozen
budgets --- yields the same choice in 9 of 12 settings, a gain in 2
(Austin, scarce and moderate: validation MAE prefers the pooled model
there, the decision criterion prefers the local one, and the local
choice is better on the held-out test), and one interval-tie; it never
harmed. In San Francisco both criteria select the pooled model, which
is also the better test model there. Decision-based validation
selection is therefore a cheap, safe check rather than an established
improvement.
